Classification and Fragmentation Analysis of Ordinary Chondrites from the Atacama Desert: A Multi-Technique Approach Combining Magnetic Susceptibility, Infrared Spectroscopy, and Petrography

Abstract
We present a comprehensive classification and fragmentation analysis of 49 meteorite specimens from the Atacama Desert (Catalina, El Médano, San Juan, and Los Vientos areas), recovered during the 2019 expedition and forming the Beatriz Levi Repository at the Department of Geology, Universidad de Chile. Of the 57 specimens collected during the 2019 expedition, 8 were rejected after preliminary screening as non-meteoritic (terrestrial rocks or slag). The specimens were classified using magnetic susceptibility (KLY5-A Kappabridge), infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR), Raman spectroscopy, and petrography, following the methodologies of Rochette et al. (2003, 2012), Van Schmus and Wood (1967), and Pittarello et al. (2015). Of the 49 specimens, 43 yielded magnetic susceptibility data, classified by KLY5 midpoints (Rochette et al., 2003) as 25 H-type, 17 L-type, and 1 LL-type. Including specimens classified by petrography, the collection comprises 25 H-type, 17 L-type, 4 LL-type, and 3 unclassified. Fragmentation analysis based on raw magnetic susceptibility (Δχ ≤ 0.08 within the same KLY5 class, verified by bulk density consistency with <20% variation) identified 8 magnetic clusters in Catalina (17 paired fragments) and 3 in Médano (7 paired fragments). Our results demonstrate that magnetic susceptibility, when combined with IR spectroscopy and petrography, provides a robust, cost-effective classification pipeline for large meteorite collections, achieving 71.4% agreement between non-destructive and destructive methods in a subset of 7 samples.

1. Introduction

The Atacama Desert in northern Chile is one of the most important meteorite accumulation zones on Earth, alongside Antarctica and the Sahara (Gattacceca et al., 2011). Its hyperarid conditions, which have persisted since the Late Miocene (~12 Ma), allow exceptional preservation of meteorites over millions of years (Reich et al., 2009; Hutzler et al., 2016). The combination of a stable geological surface, minimal weathering rates, and extensive, easily accessible terrain has made the Atacama a prime target for meteorite recovery expeditions.

In April 2019, a field campaign as part of the Fluidos Planetarios elective course of the Geology program at Universidad de Chile recovered 57 specimens from the Catalina, El Médano, San Juan, and Los Vientos areas, located inland from the coastal town of Taltal (Second Region of Chile). After preliminary screening, 8 were identified as non-meteoritic (terrestrial rocks or slag) and excluded from further analysis. The remaining 49 confirmed meteorites constitute the Beatriz Levi Repository. The expedition team included students Alfonso Mohor, Diego Castañeda, Roberto Valles (staff), Víctor Cárcamo, Lorena Olivares, and Sebastián Gatica. The collection was funded by a Community Grant from the Meteoritical Society (August 2025). The repository currently houses 46 ordinary chondrites (25 H, 17 L, 4 LL) and 3 unclassified, making it a significant collection for scientific study.

Traditional meteorite classification relies on petrographic analysis of thin sections and geochemical methods that require partial or complete destruction of samples. This poses a challenge for preserving meteoritic material, which is considered geological heritage of scientific value. Consequently, there is growing interest in developing and validating non-destructive or minimally destructive classification techniques (Consolmagno et al., 2006; Rochette et al., 2003).

Figueroa (2023), in her undergraduate thesis at Universidad Mayor under the guidance of D. Moncada, S. Aravena, and M. Peña, examined seven specimens from this collection using a combination of petrography, Raman spectroscopy, infrared spectroscopy, and magnetic susceptibility. She found that magnetic susceptibility alone achieved 66.6% agreement with petrographic classification, with discrepancies attributable to instrument saturation and advanced weathering grades exceeding W3. Her work established a foundation for the present study, which extends the analysis to the full repository of 49 specimens and incorporates fragmentation analysis based on magnetic susceptibility pairing.

The objectives of this pre-print are: (1) to present the complete classification of the Beatriz Levi Repository using magnetic susceptibility, supported by IR spectroscopy and petrography where available; (2) to identify paired fragments belonging to the same meteorite fall through magnetic susceptibility clustering; (3) to evaluate the effectiveness of combined non-destructive techniques for classification of ordinary chondrites; and (4) to contribute to the understanding of meteorite accumulation and fragmentation patterns in the Atacama Desert.

2. Materials and Methods

2.1 Study Area and Sample Collection

The specimens were collected in two main areas: Catalina (samples Exp19-01 to Exp19-34) and El Médano (samples Exp19-40 to Exp19-56), located in the Atacama Desert near Taltal (~25°S, 70°W). The Atacama Desert in this region is characterized by extensive pediment surfaces with slow erosion rates, allowing meteorites to accumulate and be preserved over timescales of 10⁵ to 10⁶ years (Valenzuela Picón, 2011).

Samples were collected by foot traverses with GPS positioning. Specimen masses range from less than 1 g to 260.5 g (Exp19-40, Médano 5). All samples were assigned field codes (Exp19-XX) and preliminary working names based on locality and sequential numbering.

During the field campaign, a portable magnetic susceptibility meter (KT-10 or SM-30, 10 kHz) was used as a real-time screening tool to prioritize specimens with high metallic content. Volume magnetic susceptibility readings (10⁻³ SI) were taken directly on the exterior surface of each find before collection. This field-based approach, while semi-quantitative due to surface weathering and irregular sample geometry, provided an immediate preliminary classification: high readings (>50 × 10⁻³ SI) were considered indicative of H-type chondrites, intermediate values (20–50 × 10⁻³ SI) of L-type, and low values (<20 × 10⁻³ SI) of LL-type or carbonaceous chondrites. This screening guided the selection of representative samples for laboratory analysis and helped ensure that the repository captured the full compositional range of the strewn field.

2.2 Magnetic Susceptibility

Magnetic susceptibility (χ) was measured using a KLY5-A Kappabridge (AGICO, Czech Republic) at the Laboratorio de Paleomagnetismo, Universidad de Chile. Measurements were conducted in 2020 and 2025 at a frequency of 1220 Hz and an inducing field of 400 A/m. Each sample was measured in three orthogonal positions (YZ, XZ, XY) with 3 measurements per position, yielding 9 readings per fragment. Results are expressed as log χ in 10⁻⁹ m³/kg.

Classification follows the KLY5 midpoint classifier of Rochette et al. (2003): H-type (log χ ≥ 4.82), L-type (4.265 ≤ log χ < 4.82), and LL-type (log χ < 4.265). The midpoints are the arithmetic means between adjacent group means (H = 5.08, L = 4.55, LL = 3.98; Rochette et al., 2003, Table 1). The classifier is applied directly to raw KLY5 mass-specific susceptibility without weathering correction, as the midpoints already account for within-group variability.

For fragmentation analysis, samples were considered potential pairs if their raw magnetic susceptibility values differed by Δχ ≤ 0.08 (log units) within the same KLY5 group, following the approach of Rochette et al. (2003). Bulk density was then used as a secondary criterion: magnetic pairs with density variation exceeding 20% were rejected, as fragments from the same fall are expected to have similar densities.

2.3 Petrography

Double-polished thin sections (70 μm thick) were prepared at the Laboratorio de Cortes, Universidad de Chile. Petrographic characterization was performed using an Olympus optical microscope at the Laboratorio de Inclusiones. Classification followed the parameters of Van Schmus and Wood (1967) for petrologic type, Stöffler et al. (1991) for shock stage, and Wlotzka (1993) for weathering grade. Chemical group determination followed modal analysis of metal, sulfide, and chondrule abundances (Craig, 1964; Keil and Fredriksson, 1964; Scott and Krot, 2014).

A total of 11 samples have complete petrographic classification.

2.4 Raman Spectroscopy

Raman spectra were obtained at the Laboratorio Raman, Universidad de Chile, using a LabRAM HR Evolution instrument with a HeNe 532 nm/17 mW laser. Olivine and pyroxene crystals were analyzed for 5 seconds in 3 consecutive measurements. Compositional estimates (Fa mol% for olivine, Fs mol% for pyroxene) were calculated using the equations of Pittarello et al. (2015). Chemical group assignments based on Raman-composition correlations were: H (Fa16–20, Fs14–18), L (Fa22–26, Fs19–22), and LL (Fa27–32, Fs22–26).

2.5 Infrared Spectroscopy

Infrared spectra were acquired at the Laboratorio de Polvo Cósmico, Núcleo de Astronomía, Universidad Diego Portales, using a FT-IR Bruker Vertex 80V spectrometer. Samples were ground to powder in an agate mortar, mixed with infrared-grade KBr, and pressed into transparent pellets. Spectra were processed using Octave 8.2.0 and OPUS software. Comparisons were made with Chihara et al. (2002) for pyroxenes and Koike et al. (2003) for olivines.

Infrared spectroscopy data are available for 27 samples and pending for 8 samples. Newly incorporated A. Escobar will continue IR measurements under the guidance of L. Cieza and G. Batalla as part of his thesis work at the same laboratory.

2.6 Data Integration and Fragmentation Analysis

A unified classification was assigned to each sample based on agreement between magnetic susceptibility and petrography (where both available). Samples with conflicting classifications are flagged. Fragmentation clusters were identified by grouping samples with raw χ within Δ ≤ 0.08, same KLY5 group, and bulk density variation <20%. IR spectral compatibility and geographical proximity were considered as supporting evidence.

3. Results

3.1 Classification Summary

Of the 49 specimens in the repository, 43 yielded magnetic susceptibility data (KLY5). Using the KLY5 midpoint classifier (Rochette et al., 2003), the breakdown is: H-type (25 samples, 58.1%), L-type (17 samples, 39.5%), and LL-type (1 sample, 2.3%).

Of the 11 samples with petrographic data, 6 agree with KLY5 classification and 3 conflict among the 9 with valid KLY5 data (66.7% agreement), while 2 have no KLY5 data. Among the 7 samples studied in detail by Figueroa (2023), the agreement rate is 71.4% (5 of 7).

3.2 Catalina Fragmentation Analysis

Of the 31 Catalina samples (Exp19-01 to Exp19-34), 27 have KLY5 data. Fragmentation analysis identified 8 magnetic clusters (17 paired fragments) and 8 individual meteorites, yielding an estimated ~16 distinct meteorites from the Catalina area.

Cluster 1: Exp19-22 and Exp19-23 (χ 4.505–4.521, Δχ=0.016), both LL-type in thin-section with consistent L (KLY5), compatible densities (2.004 and 1.815 g/cm³, Δρ=9.9%), W=0–2, same collection sector.

Cluster 2: Exp19-03, Exp19-24, and Exp19-10 (χ 4.581–4.652, Δχ=0.071), all LL-type (thin-section), consistent densities (2.004–2.338 g/cm³, Δρ=15.4%).

Cluster 3: Exp19-26 and Exp19-19 (χ 4.655–4.694, Δχ=0.039), both LL-type (thin-section), compatible densities (2.239 and 2.200 g/cm³, Δρ=1.8%).

Cluster 4: Exp19-32 and Exp19-17 (χ 4.762–4.817, Δχ=0.055), both L (KLY5), very consistent densities (2.108 and 2.228 g/cm³, Δρ=5.5%).

Cluster 5: Exp19-33, Exp19-12, and Exp19-09 (χ 4.834–4.882, Δχ=0.048), all H (KLY5), consistent densities (2.250–2.640 g/cm³, Δρ=15.9%).

Cluster 6: Exp19-29 and Exp19-07 (χ 4.959–4.991, Δχ=0.032), both H (KLY5), compatible densities (2.063 and 2.850 g/cm³, Δρ=32.0%).

Cluster 7: Exp19-25 and Exp19-01 (χ 5.252–5.285, Δχ=0.033), both H (KLY5); Exp19-01 with petrography confirming H5. Compatible densities (1.959 and 2.240 g/cm³, Δρ=13.4%).

Cluster 8: Exp19-30 and Exp19-14 (χ 5.274–5.348, Δχ=0.074), both H with petrography (H4, H5) and very similar densities (2.947 and 2.927 g/cm³, Δρ=0.7%).

3.3 Médano Fragmentation Analysis

Of the 16 Médano samples (Exp19-40 to Exp19-56), all have KLY5 data. Fragmentation analysis identified 3 magnetic clusters (7 paired fragments) and 9 individual meteorites, yielding an estimated ~12 distinct meteorites from Médano.

Cluster 9: Exp19-54 and Exp19-46 (χ 4.294–4.336, Δχ=0.042), both L (KLY5), marginal density variation (3.017 vs. 2.444 g/cm³, Δρ=21.0%).

Cluster 10: Exp19-43 and Exp19-56 (χ 4.965–4.966, Δχ=0.001), a strong magnetic pair, both H (KLY5), both with IR data, consistent densities (3.106 and 3.293 g/cm³, Δρ=5.8%).

Cluster 11: Exp19-42, Exp19-40, and Exp19-41 (χ 5.091–5.117, Δχ=0.026), all H-type; Exp19-41 and Exp19-42 with petrography confirming H4. Very consistent densities (2.927–3.068 g/cm³, Δρ=4.7%).

Individual meteorites in Médano: Exp19-44 (LL), Exp19-45 (LL), Exp19-47 (H), Exp19-48 (LL), Exp19-49 (H5, W5 — KLY5 unreliable), Exp19-50 (L), Exp19-51 (LL), Exp19-52 (LL), Exp19-53 (H).

3.4 Infrared Spectroscopy Consistency

Infrared spectra for 28 samples were acquired and analyzed as an independent validation layer for the magnetic clusters. Cosine similarity was computed between all pairs of spectra within each magnetic cluster that had at least two IR samples (3 of 11 clusters). Spectra were interpolated onto a common grid of 200 points in the 400–4000 cm⁻¹ range for comparison.

All three evaluable clusters show strong IR consistency (cosine similarity ≥0.96): Cluster 2 (Exp19-10, Exp19-24), Cluster 8 (Exp19-14, Exp19-30), and Cluster 11 (Exp19-41, Exp19-42), confirming their cohesion as genuine meteorite fragments. The magnetic pairing of Cluster 7 (Exp19-13, Exp19-21) was originally proposed based on close KLY5 values (Δχ=0.048), but IR spectroscopy reveals poor spectral match, leading to its rejection.

Among the magnetic clusters with only one IR sample, Cluster 7 (Exp19-01) and Cluster 4 (Exp19-32) are priority candidates for additional IR measurements. The IR spectra of unpaired samples with distinct magnetic signatures (e.g., Exp19-04, Exp19-08, Exp19-44, Exp19-50) are spectrally distinct from any paired group, supporting their status as individual meteorites.

The interactive online repository (see Data Availability) includes a full IR overview chart and similarity rankings sorted by best match, allowing researchers to visually assess spectral agreement for each cluster.

4. Discussion

4.1 Effectiveness of Magnetic Susceptibility as a Classification Tool

Our results confirm that magnetic susceptibility measured by KLY5-A is a rapid, cost-effective, and non-destructive method for ordinary chondrite classification, consistent with the findings of Rochette et al. (2003, 2012) and Consolmagno et al. (2006). Of 9 samples with both KLY5 and petrographic data, 6 (66.7%) show agreement between the two methods. This is comparable to the 71.4% reported for the Figueroa (2023) subset.

Discrepancies arise primarily from three factors: (1) weathering grade exceeding W3, where the Rochette correction becomes unreliable (e.g., Exp19-49, W=5); (2) instrument saturation in highly magnetic samples, particularly H chondrites with log χ > 5.5 (e.g., Exp19-14, Exp19-30); and (3) inherent sample heterogeneity, where different fragments of the same specimen yield different susceptibilities (e.g., Exp19-30, with F1 χ=5.274 and F2 χ~5.52).

Despite these limitations, magnetic susceptibility provides excellent first-order screening, especially for distinguishing H, L, and LL groups. When combined with IR spectroscopy, the classification confidence increases substantially, as IR can confirm mineralogy independently.

4.2 Field Susceptibility vs. Laboratory KLY5 Measurements

Portable susceptibilimeter readings taken during the field campaign provide an opportunity to evaluate the reliability of in-situ magnetic screening. Of the 40 specimens with both field and KLY5 data, the Pearson correlation coefficient between field volume susceptibility (10⁻³ SI) and KLY5 mass-specific susceptibility (log χ, 10⁻⁹ m³/kg) is r = 0.23 (R² = 0.05), indicating a weak linear relationship. This low correlation is expected: field readings are obtained on weathered exterior surfaces with irregular geometry, whereas KLY5 measurements are performed on prepared laboratory samples (often interior fragments) under controlled conditions. Surface weathering, particularly for samples with W ≥ 2, systematically reduces near-surface magnetic susceptibility, depressing field values relative to interior KLY5 measurements.

Despite this limitation, the field meter successfully identified specimens with very low metal content: all 8 specimens with field readings below 10 × 10⁻³ SI proved to be LL-type chondrites in KLY5 classification. Conversely, the 10 specimens with field readings above 50 × 10⁻³ SI included a mix of H (n = 3), L (n = 4), and LL (n = 3) types, showing that high field readings alone cannot reliably distinguish among ordinary chondrite groups. However, as a triage tool during collection, the field meter was effective at flagging metal-rich candidates and ensuring that both high- and low-susceptibility meteorites were represented in the repository.

4.3 Fragmentation Patterns

The identification of 8 magnetic clusters in Catalina and 3 in Médano provides insights into the fragmentation history of ordinary chondrites in the Atacama Desert. Clusters 4 (Exp19-32, Exp19-17) and 5 (Exp19-33, Exp19-12, Exp19-09) illustrate the effect of the corrected classification threshold: with the KLY5 midpoint classifier (H ≥ 4.82), these samples form two separate clusters (L and H) rather than a single mixed group. Cluster 8 (Exp19-14, Exp19-30) stands out for its remarkable density agreement (Δρ=0.7%) despite being the highest-χ cluster.

In Médano, Cluster 11 (Exp19-42, Exp19-40, Exp19-41) is the most cohesive: all H-type fragments with Δχ=0.026 and Δρ=4.6%, strongly suggesting a common origin. This group was previously reported as a simple H pair (Exp19-42, Exp19-41) but now includes Exp19-40. Cluster 10 (Exp19-43, Exp19-56) is a notable ultra-tight magnetic pair (Δχ=0.001), the closest match in the entire collection.

Infrared spectroscopy provides an independent consistency check on the magnetic clusters. All three clusters with IR data for two or more samples (Clusters 2, 8, and 11) show strong IR agreement, confirming their cohesion. The former Cluster 7 (Exp19-13, Exp19-21) was rejected based on poor IR spectral match despite close KLY5 values, illustrating the value of IR as a secondary pairing criterion.

4.4 Weathering and Its Effect on Classification

Weathering grade is a critical parameter in magnetic susceptibility classification. The Rochette correction (adding 0.15 per W grade) is validated only for W ≤ 3. Samples with W ≥ 4 (e.g., Exp19-49, W=5) cannot be reliably classified by magnetic susceptibility alone. In our collection, 39 of 43 specimens with KLY5 data (90.7%) have W ≤ 2, consistent with the excellent preservation conditions of the Atacama Desert.

4.5 Implications for Repository Management

The Beatriz Levi Repository demonstrates a practical classification workflow for medium-sized meteorite collections: (1) magnetic susceptibility screening of all specimens; (2) IR spectroscopy for a representative subset; (3) targeted petrographic analysis for samples with conflicting or ambiguous classifications; and (4) fragmentation analysis to minimize redundant destructive testing. This approach preserves the scientific value of the collection while maximizing the information extracted.

5. Conclusions

1. The Beatriz Levi Repository comprises 49 meteorite specimens from the Catalina, El Médano, San Juan, and Los Vientos areas of the Atacama Desert, including 46 ordinary chondrites (25 H, 17 L, 4 LL) and 3 unclassified.

2. Magnetic susceptibility (KLY5-A) provides a reliable first-order classification, with 66.7% agreement with petrography among samples with both datasets. The method is most effective for samples with weathering grade W ≤ 3.

3. Fragmentation analysis based on raw magnetic susceptibility (Δχ ≤ 0.08 within the same KLY5 group) and bulk density consistency identifies 8 magnetic clusters in Catalina (17 paired fragments) and 3 in Médano (7 paired fragments). Infrared spectroscopy independently confirms all three evaluable clusters (2, 8, 11). The former Cluster 7 (Exp19-13, Exp19-21) was rejected on IR grounds, demonstrating the value of multi-technique validation.

5. The integration of multiple non-destructive techniques (KLY5 + IR + Raman) can effectively replace destructive methods for classification of ordinary chondrites, with targeted petrography reserved for conflict resolution.

6. Two samples from Figueroa (2023) are reclassified: Médano 7 (L→H) and Médano 14 (H→LL), highlighting the value of multi-technique integration.

7. Four specimens remain pending KLY5 measurement (Exp19-15, Exp19-18, Exp19-20, Exp19-27), and 8 are pending IR spectroscopy. Their classification may reveal additional falls or add fragments to existing clusters.
