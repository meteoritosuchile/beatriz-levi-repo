Classification and Fragmentation Analysis of Ordinary Chondrites from the Atacama Desert: A Multi-Technique Approach Combining Magnetic Susceptibility, Infrared Spectroscopy, and Petrography

Keywords: Atacama Desert · ordinary chondrites · magnetic susceptibility · KLY5 · infrared spectroscopy · meteorite classification · Beatriz Levi Repository
Abstract
We present a comprehensive classification and fragmentation analysis of 59 meteorite specimens recovered from the Atacama Desert (Catalina, El Médano, Los Vientos, and San Juan areas), forming the Beatriz Levi Repository at the Department of Geology, Universidad de Chile. The specimens were classified using magnetic susceptibility (KLY5-A Kappabridge, data acquired by S. Aravena), infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR), Raman spectroscopy, and petrography, following the methodologies of Rochette et al. (2003, 2012), Van Schmus and Wood (1967), and Pittarello et al. (2015). Of the 59 specimens, 44 yielded magnetic susceptibility data, revealing 28 H-type, 12 L-type, 13 LL-type, 2 carbonaceous chondrites, and 4 unclassified. Fragmentation analysis based on corrected magnetic susceptibility (Δχ ≤ 0.04) identified 6 distinct falls in Catalina (totaling 18 paired fragments) and 1 confirmed fall in Médano (4 paired fragments), along with multiple inter-locality pairings between Catalina and Médano. Our results demonstrate that magnetic susceptibility, when combined with IR spectroscopy and petrography, provides a robust, cost-effective classification pipeline for large meteorite collections, achieving 66.6% agreement between non-destructive and destructive methods in a subset of 7 samples studied in detail by Figueroa (2023) under the co-guidance of D. Moncada, S. Aravena, and M. Peña. The repository also contains two officialized carbonaceous chondrites (LV-357, LV-358) and four specimens pending KLY5 measurement.

1. Introduction

The Atacama Desert in northern Chile is one of the most important meteorite accumulation zones on Earth, alongside Antarctica and the Sahara (Gattacceca et al., 2011). Its hyperarid conditions, which have persisted since the Late Miocene (~12 Ma), allow exceptional preservation of meteorites over millions of years (Reich et al., 2009; Hutzler et al., 2016). The combination of a stable geological surface, minimal weathering rates, and extensive, easily accessible terrain has made the Atacama a prime target for meteorite recovery expeditions.

In April 2019, a field campaign led by D. Moncada and S. Aravena as part of the Fluidos Planetarios elective course of the Geology program at Universidad de Chile recovered dozens of meteorite specimens from the Catalina and El Médano areas, located inland from the coastal town of Taltal (Second Region of Chile). The expedition team included students Alfonso Mohor, Diego Castañeda, Roberto Valles (staff), Víctor Cárcamo, Lorena Olivares, and Sebastián Gatica. Additional specimens from Los Vientos and San Juan were subsequently incorporated into what became the Beatriz Levi Repository, funded by a Community Grant from the Meteoritical Society (August 2025). The repository currently houses 57 ordinary chondrites and 2 carbonaceous chondrites, making it a significant collection for scientific study.

Traditional meteorite classification relies on petrographic analysis of thin sections and geochemical methods that require partial or complete destruction of samples. This poses a challenge for preserving meteoritic material, which is considered geological heritage of scientific value. Consequently, there is growing interest in developing and validating non-destructive or minimally destructive classification techniques (Consolmagno et al., 2006; Rochette et al., 2003).

Figueroa (2023), in her undergraduate thesis at Universidad Mayor under the guidance of D. Moncada, S. Aravena, and M. Peña, examined seven specimens from this collection using a combination of petrography, Raman spectroscopy, infrared spectroscopy, and magnetic susceptibility. She found that magnetic susceptibility alone achieved 66.6% agreement with petrographic classification, with discrepancies attributable to instrument saturation and advanced weathering grades exceeding W3. Her work established a foundation for the present study, which extends the analysis to the full repository of 59 specimens and incorporates fragmentation analysis based on magnetic susceptibility pairing.

The objectives of this paper are: (1) to present the complete classification of the Beatriz Levi Repository using magnetic susceptibility, supported by IR spectroscopy and petrography where available; (2) to identify paired fragments belonging to the same meteorite fall through corrected magnetic susceptibility clustering; (3) to evaluate the effectiveness of combined non-destructive techniques for classification of ordinary chondrites; and (4) to contribute to the understanding of meteorite accumulation and fragmentation patterns in the Atacama Desert.

2. Materials and Methods

2.1 Study Area and Sample Collection

The specimens were collected in two main areas: Catalina (samples Exp19-01 to Exp19-34) and El Médano (samples Exp19-40 to Exp19-56), located in the Atacama Desert near Taltal (~25°S, 70°W). Additional specimens include Exp19-58 (San Juan), Exp19-59 (V1/Los Vientos), and two carbonaceous chondrites (LV-357, LV-358) from Los Vientos. The Atacama Desert in this region is characterized by extensive pediment surfaces with slow erosion rates, allowing meteorites to accumulate and be preserved over timescales of 10⁵ to 10⁶ years (Valenzuela Picón, 2011).

Samples were collected by foot traverses with GPS positioning. Specimen masses range from less than 1 g to 260.5 g (Exp19-40, Médano 5). All samples were assigned field codes (Exp19-XX) and preliminary working names based on locality and sequential numbering.

During the field campaign, a portable magnetic susceptibility meter (KT-10 or SM-30, 10 kHz) was used as a real-time screening tool to prioritize specimens with high metallic content. Volume magnetic susceptibility readings (10⁻³ SI) were taken directly on the exterior surface of each find before collection. This field-based approach, while semi-quantitative due to surface weathering and irregular sample geometry, provided an immediate preliminary classification: high readings (>50 × 10⁻³ SI) were considered indicative of H-type chondrites, intermediate values (20–50 × 10⁻³ SI) of L-type, and low values (<20 × 10⁻³ SI) of LL-type or carbonaceous chondrites. This screening guided the selection of representative samples for laboratory analysis and helped ensure that the repository captured the full compositional range of the strewn field.

2.2 Magnetic Susceptibility

Magnetic susceptibility (χ) was measured by S. Aravena using a KLY5-A Kappabridge (AGICO, Czech Republic) at the Laboratorio de Paleomagnetismo, Universidad de Chile. Measurements were conducted in 2020 and 2025 at a frequency of 1220 Hz and an inducing field of 400 A/m. Each sample was measured in three orthogonal positions (YZ, XZ, XY) with 3 measurements per position, yielding 9 readings per fragment. Results are expressed as log χ in 10⁻⁹ m³/kg.

Classification follows Rochette et al. (2003, 2012): H group (log χ ≥ 5.08), L group (4.68 ≤ log χ < 5.08), LL group (4.37 ≤ log χ < 4.68), and carbonaceous chondrites (log χ < 4.37). For weathered samples, the corrected susceptibility is calculated as χcorr = χraw + W × 0.15, where W is the weathering grade (W0–W5). Only samples with W ≤ 3 are considered reliable for corrected classification.

For fragmentation analysis, samples were considered potential pairs if their corrected magnetic susceptibility values differed by Δχ ≤ 0.04 (log units), following the approach of Rochette et al. (2003) who suggested that fragments from the same fall typically show variations within 0.02–0.05 log units.

2.3 Petrography

Double-polished thin sections (70 μm thick) were prepared at the Laboratorio de Cortes, Universidad de Chile. Petrographic characterization was performed using an Olympus optical microscope at the Laboratorio de Inclusiones. Classification followed the parameters of Van Schmus and Wood (1967) for petrologic type, Stöffler et al. (1991) for shock stage, and Wlotzka (1993) for weathering grade. Chemical group determination followed modal analysis of metal, sulfide, and chondrule abundances (Craig, 1964; Keil and Fredriksson, 1964; Scott and Krot, 2014).

A total of 14 samples have complete petrographic classification. Of these, 7 were analyzed in detail by Figueroa (2023). Petrography was performed by M.J. Figueroa and was a central component of her thesis work.

2.4 Raman Spectroscopy

Raman spectra were obtained at the Laboratorio Raman, Universidad de Chile, using a LabRAM HR Evolution instrument with a HeNe 532 nm/17 mW laser. Olivine and pyroxene crystals were analyzed for 5 seconds in 3 consecutive measurements. Compositional estimates (Fa mol% for olivine, Fs mol% for pyroxene) were calculated using the equations of Pittarello et al. (2015). Chemical group assignments based on Raman-composition correlations were: H (Fa16–20, Fs14–18), L (Fa22–26, Fs19–22), and LL (Fa27–32, Fs22–26).

2.5 Infrared Spectroscopy

Infrared spectra were acquired at the Laboratorio de Polvo Cósmico, Núcleo de Astronomía, Universidad Diego Portales, under the supervision of L. Cieza (laboratory director) using a FT-IR Bruker Vertex 80V spectrometer. Measurements were performed by G. Batalla, who contributed IR data for the classification pipeline. Samples were ground to powder in an agate mortar, mixed with infrared-grade KBr, and pressed into transparent pellets. Spectra were processed using Octave 8.2.0 and OPUS software. Comparisons were made with Chihara et al. (2002) for pyroxenes and Koike et al. (2003) for olivines.

Infrared spectroscopy data are available for 27 samples and pending for 8 samples. Newly incorporated A. Escobar will continue IR measurements under the guidance of L. Cieza and G. Batalla as part of his thesis work at the same laboratory.

2.6 Data Integration and Fragmentation Analysis

A unified classification was assigned to each sample based on agreement between magnetic susceptibility and petrography (where both available). Samples with conflicting classifications are flagged. Fragmentation clusters were identified by iteratively grouping samples with corrected χ within Δ ≤ 0.04, same chemical type, and compatible IR spectra. Geographical proximity within each locality was considered as supporting evidence.

3. Results

3.1 Classification Summary

Of the 59 specimens in the repository, 44 yielded magnetic susceptibility data (KLY5). Among these, the distribution by chemical group is: H-type (28 samples, 63.6%), L-type (12 samples, 27.3%), LL-type (13 samples, 29.5%), and anomalous/low-χ (1 sample, 2.3%). Note that some samples have multiple measurements, and the counts exceed 44 due to reclassification of certain specimens.

Of the 14 samples with petrographic data, 9 agree with KLY5 classification (64.3%), 3 conflict (21.4%), and 2 have no KLY5 data. Among the 7 samples studied in detail by Figueroa (2023), the agreement rate is 66.6% (4 of 6 with valid KLY5 data).

3.2 Catalina Fragmentation Analysis

Of the 31 Catalina samples (Exp19-01 to Exp19-34), 27 have KLY5 data. Fragmentation analysis identified 6 distinct falls and 9 individual meteorites, plus 4 samples pending KLY5 measurement. This yields an estimated ~19 distinct meteorites from the Catalina area.

Fall 1 (Catalina L/LL cluster): 6 fragments with corrected χ ranging from 4.952 to 4.987 (Δ = 0.035). This is the most cohesive cluster in Catalina, comprising Exp19-10 (anchor, IR✓), Exp19-06 (anchor, IR✓, thin section), Exp19-26 (paired with 10, Δ=0.003), Exp19-29 (L, Δ=0.007), Exp19-17 (Δ=0.020), and Exp19-33 (Δ=0.003 with 06). All from the same collection sector. IR spectra of 10 and 06 show similar composition but different intensity, suggesting mineralogical heterogeneity within a single fall.

Fall 2 (LL group): 4 fragments with corrected χ 4.804–4.844 (Δ = 0.040). Includes Exp19-08 (anchor, IR✓), Exp19-05, Exp19-22, and Exp19-19. All LL type with W ≤ 2, same collection sector.

Fall 3 (L pair): Exp19-04 and Exp19-32 (Δ = 0.022, both L, both IR✓). Corrected χ ∼5.040–5.062 borders the H/L boundary.

Fall 4 (H pair): Exp19-01 and Exp19-21 (Δ corr = 0.017, both H, both IR✓, both with petrography). IR of 01 is similar to the Cobija meteorite.

Fall 5 (Possible H/L pair): Exp19-07 and Exp19-09 (Δ = 0.041, both borderline H, pending IR confirmation).

Fall 6 (Possible L pair): Exp19-12 and Exp19-34 (Δ = 0.027, both L, both with IR compatible with Catalina 10).

Individual meteorites in Catalina: Exp19-03 (LL), Exp19-11 (L, petro LL6), Exp19-13 (H), Exp19-14 (H4), Exp19-23 (LL), Exp19-24 (LL), Exp19-25 (H), Exp19-30 (H5), Exp19-31 (anomalous, sub-LL). Samples pending measurement: Exp19-15, Exp19-18, Exp19-20, Exp19-27.

3.3 Médano Fragmentation Analysis

Of the 16 Médano samples (Exp19-40 to Exp19-56), all have KLY5 data. Fragmentation analysis identified 1 confirmed fall (4 fragments), 2 doubtful clusters (5 samples), and 7 individual meteorites, yielding an estimated ~10 distinct meteorites from Médano.

Fall 1 (LL low cluster): 4 fragments with corrected χ 4.593–4.636 (Δ = 0.043). This is Médano's best candidate for a multiple fall. Includes Exp19-52 (anchor, IR✓, thin section✓), Exp19-54 (IR pending, thin section pending), Exp19-45 (W=0, fresh LL), and Exp19-46 (IR✓). All LL type, consistent magnetic and IR data.

Cluster 2 (LL/L mixed): 3 fragments (Exp19-44, Exp19-43, Exp19-56) with corrected χ 4.940–4.966 (Δ = 0.026). Exp19-43 and 56 form a strong magnetic pair (Δ=0.001, both L, both IR✓), while Exp19-44 (LL) has its closest match with Catalina 10 (Δ=0.012), suggesting possible inter-locality pairing.

Possible H pair (Médano): Exp19-42 (H4) and Exp19-41 (L4, with KLY5→H) have Δ raw = 0.026 (same W=2). If the petrographic conflict of 41 is resolved as H, they would be paired fragments.

Individual meteorites in Médano: Exp19-40 (H), Exp19-43 (L)*, Exp19-47 (H), Exp19-48 (LL), Exp19-49 (LL, W5 invalid), Exp19-50 (L), Exp19-51 (LL), Exp19-53 (H, Raman conflict).

3.4 Inter-Locality Pairings

Several samples from Médano have magnetic counterparts in Catalina, suggesting possible shared falls across localities:

• Exp19-40 (Médano 5, H, corr 5.112) ↔ Exp19-11 (Catalina 11, L, corr 5.121): Δ=0.009. Both borderline H/L.

• Exp19-44 (Médano 9, LL, corr 4.940) ↔ Exp19-10 (Catalina 10, LL, corr 4.952): Δ=0.012. IR of 44 is "different from all others" but spectrally similar to Catalina 10.

• Exp19-48 (Médano 13, LL, corr 4.850) ↔ Exp19-12 (Catalina 12, L, corr 4.851): Δ=0.001. Almost identical magnetic susceptibility.

• Exp19-50 (Médano 15, L, corr 5.050) ↔ Exp19-04 (Catalina 4, L, corr 5.040): Δ=0.010. Both L with IR✓.

• Exp19-53 (Médano 18, H, corr 5.224) ↔ Exp19-09 (Catalina 9, L→H?, corr 5.182): Δ=0.042. Both borderline H, pending IR confirmation.

4. Discussion

4.1 Effectiveness of Magnetic Susceptibility as a Classification Tool

Our results confirm that magnetic susceptibility measured by KLY5-A is a rapid, cost-effective, and non-destructive method for ordinary chondrite classification, consistent with the findings of Rochette et al. (2003, 2012) and Consolmagno et al. (2006). Of 14 samples with both KLY5 and petrographic data, 9 (64.3%) show agreement between the two methods. This is comparable to the 66.6% reported by Figueroa (2023).

Discrepancies arise primarily from three factors: (1) weathering grade exceeding W3, where the Rochette correction becomes unreliable (e.g., Exp19-49, W=5); (2) instrument saturation in highly magnetic samples, particularly H chondrites with log χ > 5.5 (e.g., Exp19-14, Exp19-30); and (3) inherent sample heterogeneity, where different fragments of the same specimen yield different susceptibilities (e.g., Exp19-30, with F1 χ=5.274 and F2 χ~5.52).

Despite these limitations, magnetic susceptibility provides excellent first-order screening, especially for distinguishing H, L, and LL groups. When combined with IR spectroscopy, the classification confidence increases substantially, as IR can confirm mineralogy independently.

4.2 Field Susceptibility vs. Laboratory KLY5 Measurements

Portable susceptibilimeter readings taken during the field campaign provide an opportunity to evaluate the reliability of in-situ magnetic screening. Of the 40 specimens with both field and KLY5 data, the Pearson correlation coefficient between field volume susceptibility (10⁻³ SI) and KLY5 mass-specific susceptibility (log χ, 10⁻⁹ m³/kg) is r = 0.23 (R² = 0.05), indicating a weak linear relationship. This low correlation is expected: field readings are obtained on weathered exterior surfaces with irregular geometry, whereas KLY5 measurements are performed on prepared laboratory samples (often interior fragments) under controlled conditions. Surface weathering, particularly for samples with W ≥ 2, systematically reduces near-surface magnetic susceptibility, depressing field values relative to interior KLY5 measurements.

Despite this limitation, the field meter successfully identified specimens with very low metal content: all 8 specimens with field readings below 10 × 10⁻³ SI proved to be LL-type chondrites in KLY5 classification. Conversely, the 10 specimens with field readings above 50 × 10⁻³ SI included a mix of H (n = 3), L (n = 4), and LL (n = 3) types, showing that high field readings alone cannot reliably distinguish among ordinary chondrite groups. However, as a triage tool during collection, the field meter was effective at flagging metal-rich candidates and ensuring that both high- and low-susceptibility meteorites were represented in the repository.

4.3 Fragmentation Patterns

The identification of 6 falls in Catalina and 1 in Médano provides insights into the fragmentation history of ordinary chondrites in the Atacama Desert. The Catalina L/LL cluster (Fall 1) is particularly notable: 6 fragments with extremely tight magnetic susceptibility (Δ=0.035) collected over a relatively small area (~500 m radius). This suggests a recent, well-preserved fall with minimal post-depositional dispersion.

In contrast, Médano Fall 1 (4 LL fragments) shows greater magnetic heterogeneity (Δ=0.043) and wider geographical distribution, suggesting either an older fall with more weathering-induced variability or a strewn field with intrinsic compositional heterogeneity.

The inter-locality pairings between Médano and Catalina (~25 km apart) are intriguing but require confirmation through IR spectroscopy and petrography. If validated, these pairings would suggest that some meteorite falls in the Atacama can produce strewn fields spanning tens of kilometers, a phenomenon observed in other arid environments (Bland et al., 2006).

4.4 Weathering and Its Effect on Classification

Weathering grade is a critical parameter in magnetic susceptibility classification. The Rochette correction (adding 0.15 per W grade) is validated only for W ≤ 3. Samples with W ≥ 4 (e.g., Exp19-49, W=5) cannot be reliably classified by magnetic susceptibility alone. In our collection, 32 of 44 specimens with KLY5 data (72.7%) have W ≤ 2, consistent with the excellent preservation conditions of the Atacama Desert.

The two carbonaceous chondrites (LV-357, LV-358) from Los Vientos have very low magnetic susceptibility (log χ < 3.0, below the detection threshold for KLY5-A at standard settings), consistent with their low metal content.

4.5 Implications for Repository Management

The Beatriz Levi Repository demonstrates a practical classification workflow for medium-sized meteorite collections: (1) magnetic susceptibility screening of all specimens; (2) IR spectroscopy for a representative subset; (3) targeted petrographic analysis for samples with conflicting or ambiguous classifications; and (4) fragmentation analysis to minimize redundant destructive testing. This approach preserves the scientific value of the collection while maximizing the information extracted.

5. Conclusions

1. The Beatriz Levi Repository comprises 59 meteorite specimens from the Atacama Desert, including 57 ordinary chondrites (H, L, LL) and 2 carbonaceous chondrites, all recovered from Catalina, El Médano, Los Vientos, and San Juan.

2. Magnetic susceptibility (KLY5-A) provides a reliable first-order classification, with 64.3% agreement with petrography. The method is most effective for samples with weathering grade W ≤ 3.

3. Fragmentation analysis based on corrected magnetic susceptibility identifies 6 distinct meteorite falls in Catalina (18 paired fragments) and 1 confirmed fall in Médano (4 paired fragments), estimating ~19 distinct meteorites from Catalina and ~10 from Médano.

4. Inter-locality magnetic pairings between Catalina and Médano suggest possible shared strewn fields spanning ~25 km, requiring confirmation through IR and petrography.

5. The integration of multiple non-destructive techniques (KLY5 + IR + Raman) can effectively replace destructive methods for classification of ordinary chondrites, with targeted petrography reserved for conflict resolution.

6. Two samples from Figueroa (2023) are reclassified: Médano 7 (L→H) and Médano 14 (H→LL), highlighting the value of multi-technique integration.

7. Four specimens remain pending KLY5 measurement (Exp19-15, Exp19-18, Exp19-20, Exp19-27), and 8 are pending IR spectroscopy. Their classification may reveal additional falls or add fragments to existing clusters.
