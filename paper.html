Abstract
We present a comprehensive classification and fragmentation analysis of 49 meteorite specimens from the Atacama Desert (Catalina, El Médano, San Juan, and Los Vientos areas), recovered during the 2019 expedition and forming the Beatriz Levi Repository at the Department of Geology, Universidad de Chile. Of the 57 specimens collected during the 2019 expedition, 8 were rejected after preliminary screening as non-meteoritic (terrestrial rocks or slag). The specimens were classified using magnetic susceptibility (KLY5-A Kappabridge), infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR), Raman spectroscopy, and petrography, following the methodologies of Rochette et al. (2003, 2012), Van Schmus and Wood (1967), and Pittarello et al. (2015). Of the 49 specimens with KLY5 data, 43 yielded magnetic susceptibility data, classified by KLY5 midpoints (Rochette et al., 2003) as 20 H-type, 22 L-type, and 1 LL-type. Integrating petrography and IR spectroscopy, the collection comprises 13 H-type, 14 L-type, 18 LL-type, 4 unclassified, and 1 anomalous. Fragmentation analysis based on raw magnetic susceptibility (Δχ ≤ 0.08 within the same KLY5 class, verified by bulk density consistency with <20% variation) identified 6 magnetic clusters in Médano (15 paired fragments) and 5 in Catalina (19 paired fragments), yielding an estimated ~7 distinct meteorites from Médano and ~17 from Catalina. IR spectroscopy independently confirms all clusters with ≥2 IR samples. Our results demonstrate that magnetic susceptibility, when combined with IR spectroscopy and petrography, provides a robust, cost-effective classification pipeline for large meteorite collections.

1. Introduction

The Atacama Desert in northern Chile is one of the most important meteorite accumulation zones on Earth, alongside Antarctica and the Sahara (Gattacceca et al., 2011). Its hyperarid conditions, which have persisted since the Late Miocene (~12 Ma), allow exceptional preservation of meteorites over millions of years (Reich et al., 2009; Hutzler et al., 2016). The combination of a stable geological surface, minimal weathering rates, and extensive, easily accessible terrain has made the Atacama a prime target for meteorite recovery expeditions.

In April 2019, a field campaign as part of the Fluidos Planetarios elective course of the Geology program at Universidad de Chile recovered 57 specimens from the Catalina, El Médano, San Juan, and Los Vientos areas, located inland from the coastal town of Taltal (Second Region of Chile). After preliminary screening, 8 were identified as non-meteoritic (terrestrial rocks or slag) and excluded from further analysis. The remaining 49 confirmed meteorites constitute the Beatriz Levi Repository. The expedition team included students Alfonso Mohor, Diego Castañeda, Roberto Valles (staff), Víctor Cárcamo, Lorena Olivares, and Sebastián Gatica. The collection was funded by a Community Grant from the Meteoritical Society (August 2025). Integrating KLY5, petrography, and IR spectroscopy, the repository comprises 13 H-type, 14 L-type, 18 LL-type, 4 unclassified, and 1 anomalous ordinary chondrite, making it a significant collection for scientific study.

Traditional meteorite classification relies on petrographic analysis of thin sections and geochemical methods that require partial or complete destruction of samples. This poses a challenge for preserving meteoritic material, which is considered geological heritage of scientific value. Consequently, there is growing interest in developing and validating non-destructive or minimally destructive classification techniques (Consolmagno et al., 2006; Rochette et al., 2003).

Petrographic analysis of seven specimens (Exp19-14, Exp19-21, Exp19-30, Exp19-41, Exp19-42, Exp19-49, Exp19-53) was carried out using optical microscopy, Raman spectroscopy, and infrared spectroscopy. These petrographic observations (petrologic type, shock stage, weathering grade) and Raman mineral chemistry data provide essential input for the integrated classification. This petrographic foundation is extended in the present study to the full repository of 49 specimens, incorporating fragmentation analysis based on magnetic susceptibility pairing. Four additional specimens (Exp19-01, Exp19-11, Exp19-15, Exp19-27) were characterized following the same methodology.

The objectives of this pre-print are: (1) to present the complete classification of the Beatriz Levi Repository using magnetic susceptibility, supported by IR spectroscopy and petrography where available; (2) to identify paired fragments belonging to the same meteorite fall through magnetic susceptibility clustering; (3) to evaluate the effectiveness of combined non-destructive techniques for classification of ordinary chondrites; and (4) to contribute to the understanding of meteorite accumulation and fragmentation patterns in the Atacama Desert.

2. Materials and Methods

2.1 Study Area and Sample Collection

The specimens were collected in two main areas: Catalina (samples Exp19-01 to Exp19-34) and El Médano (samples Exp19-40 to Exp19-56), located in the Atacama Desert near Taltal (~25°S, 70°W). The Atacama Desert in this region is characterized by extensive pediment surfaces with slow erosion rates, allowing meteorites to accumulate and be preserved over timescales of 10⁵ to 10⁶ years (Valenzuela Picón, 2011).

Samples were collected by foot traverses with GPS positioning. Specimen masses range from less than 1 g to 260.5 g (Exp19-40, Médano 5). All samples were assigned field codes (Exp19-XX) and preliminary working names based on locality and sequential numbering.

During the field campaign, a portable magnetic susceptibility meter (KT-10 or SM-30, 10 kHz) was used as a real-time screening tool to prioritize specimens with high metallic content. Volume magnetic susceptibility readings (10⁻³ SI) were taken directly on the exterior surface of each find before collection. This field-based approach, while semi-quantitative due to surface weathering and irregular sample geometry, provided an immediate preliminary classification: high readings (>50 × 10⁻³ SI) were considered indicative of H-type chondrites, intermediate values (20–50 × 10⁻³ SI) of L-type, and low values (<20 × 10⁻³ SI) of LL-type or carbonaceous chondrites. This screening guided the selection of representative samples for laboratory analysis and helped ensure that the repository captured the full compositional range of the strewn field.

2.2 Magnetic Susceptibility

Magnetic susceptibility (χ) was measured using a KLY5-A Kappabridge (AGICO, Czech Republic) at the Laboratorio de Paleomagnetismo, Universidad de Chile. Measurements were conducted in 2020 and 2025 at a frequency of 1220 Hz and an inducing field of 400 A/m. Each sample was measured in three orthogonal positions (YZ, XZ, XY) with 3 measurements per position, yielding 9 readings per fragment. Results are expressed as log χ in 10⁻⁹ m³/kg.

Classification follows the KLY5 midpoint classifier of Rochette et al. (2003): H-type (log χ ≥ 4.82), L-type (4.265 ≤ log χ < 4.82), and LL-type (log χ < 4.265). The midpoints are the arithmetic means between adjacent group means (H = 5.08, L = 4.55, LL = 3.98; Rochette et al., 2003, Table 1). The classifier is applied directly to raw KLY5 mass-specific susceptibility without weathering correction, as the midpoints already account for within-group variability.

For fragmentation analysis, samples were considered potential pairs if their raw magnetic susceptibility values differed by Δχ ≤ 0.08 (log units) within the same KLY5 group, following the approach of Rochette et al. (2003). Bulk density was then used as a secondary criterion: magnetic pairs with density variation exceeding 20% were rejected, as fragments from the same fall are expected to have similar densities.

2.3 Petrography

Double-polished thin sections (70 μm thick) were prepared at the Laboratorio de Cortes, Universidad de Chile. Petrographic characterization was performed using an Olympus optical microscope at the Laboratorio de Inclusiones. Classification followed the parameters of Van Schmus and Wood (1967) for petrologic type, Stöffler et al. (1991) for shock stage, and Wlotzka (1993) for weathering grade. Chemical group determination followed modal analysis of metal, sulfide, and chondrule abundances (Craig, 1964; Keil and Fredriksson, 1964; Scott and Krot, 2014).

Eleven specimens have complete petrographic classification from thin-section analysis.

2.4 Raman Spectroscopy

Raman spectra were obtained at the Laboratorio Raman, Universidad de Chile, using a LabRAM HR Evolution instrument with a HeNe 532 nm/17 mW laser. Olivine and pyroxene crystals were analyzed for 5 seconds in 3 consecutive measurements. Compositional estimates (Fa mol% for olivine, Fs mol% for pyroxene) were calculated using the equations of Pittarello et al. (2015). Chemical group assignments based on Raman-composition correlations were: H (Fa16–20, Fs14–18), L (Fa22–26, Fs19–22), and LL (Fa27–32, Fs22–26).

2.5 Infrared Spectroscopy

Infrared spectra were acquired at the Laboratorio de Polvo Cósmico, Núcleo de Astronomía, Universidad Diego Portales, using a FT-IR Bruker Vertex 80V spectrometer. Samples were ground to powder in an agate mortar, mixed with infrared-grade KBr, and pressed into transparent pellets. Spectra were processed using Octave 8.2.0 and OPUS software. Comparisons were made with Chihara et al. (2002) for pyroxenes and Koike et al. (2003) for olivines.

Infrared spectroscopy data are available for 27 samples and pending for 8 samples. Newly incorporated A. Escobar will continue IR measurements under the guidance of L. Cieza and G. Batalla as part of his thesis work at the same laboratory.

2.6 Data Integration and Fragmentation Analysis

A unified classification was assigned to each sample based on agreement between magnetic susceptibility and petrography (where both available). Samples with conflicting classifications are flagged. Fragmentation clusters were identified by grouping samples with raw χ within Δ ≤ 0.08, same KLY5 group, and bulk density variation <20%. IR spectral compatibility and geographical proximity were considered as supporting evidence.

3. Results

3.1 Classification Summary

Of the 49 specimens in the repository, 43 yielded magnetic susceptibility data (KLY5). Using the KLY5 midpoint classifier (Rochette et al., 2003), the breakdown is: H-type (20 samples, 46.5%), L-type (22 samples, 51.2%), and LL-type (1 sample, 2.3%).

Integrating petrography (11 thin-section samples), the final classification comprises 13 H-type, 14 L-type, 18 LL-type, 4 unclassified, and 1 anomalous specimen (Fig. 1).

3.2 Médano Fragmentation Analysis

All 16 Médano specimens (Exp19-40 to Exp19-56) have KLY5 data. Fragmentation analysis identified 6 magnetic clusters (15 paired fragments) and 1 individual meteorite, yielding an estimated ~7 distinct meteorites from the Médano area. Compared to Catalina, the Médano clusters show a higher proportion of paired fragments (93.8% paired vs. 71.0% in Catalina), suggesting a more concentrated strewn field with less scatter. The Médano clusters span both H-type (Clusters 2, 4) and L-type (Clusters 1, 3, 5, 6) groups.

Cluster 1 (Exp19-49, Exp19-44; χ 4.631–4.640, Δχ=0.009): Two samples with nearly identical χ values — the tightest magnetic match in the entire collection (Δχ=0.009). Both classify as L by KLY5, but Exp19-49 is petrographically H5 with weathering grade W5, at which the Rochette correction becomes unreliable (see Section 4.1). The density mismatch (Exp19-49: 2.377 vs. Exp19-44: 3.194 g/cm³, Δρ=29.5%) exceeds the 20% threshold and may reflect the advanced oxidation of Exp19-49 (oxides occupying 90% of the opaque fraction, original metal almost entirely replaced). Despite these discrepancies, IR spectra give 99.5% cosine similarity — the highest in the collection — confirming that both fragments share a common mineralogy. This pair is retained provisionally as a case where extreme weathering obscures magnetic and density classification but IR spectroscopy resolves the ambiguity.

Cluster 2 (Exp19-53, Exp19-43, Exp19-56; χ 4.924–4.966, Δχ=0.042): Three H-group samples with moderate χ spread and consistent KLY5 classification. Exp19-53 is petrographically H4, confirming the H-group assignment. Densities range 2.563–3.293 g/cm³ (Δρ=24.8%), with Exp19-56 at 3.293 being the densest Médano sample. IR spectra for Exp19-53, Exp19-43, and Exp19-56 give 98.8% average cosine similarity, confirming the pair. The three fragments span a wider area within Médano than other clusters, suggesting a larger strewn ellipse. The cluster's mean density (2.977 g/cm³) is consistent with H chondrite bulk density (~3.0–3.5 g/cm³ for unweathered falls).

Cluster 3 (Exp19-52, Exp19-51; χ 4.443–4.464, Δχ=0.021): Two L-group samples with a tight χ range and the best density match in Médano (2.365 vs. 2.622 g/cm³, Δρ=10.4%), well within the 20% threshold. IR spectra give 99.4% cosine similarity. These two fragments are likely from the same small meteorite, possibly the same individual that broke on impact. Exp19-52 consists of 23 fragments totalling 297.5 g — the largest individual mass in the collection — suggesting this meteorite was recovered nearly complete.

Cluster 4 (Exp19-42, Exp19-40, Exp19-41, Exp19-47; χ 5.091–5.168, Δχ=0.077): The largest Médano cluster with 4 fragments and the widest χ range in Médano (approaching the 0.08 threshold), all H-type. Petrography independently confirms H4 for Exp19-41 and Exp19-42. The χ progression within the cluster (Exp19-42: 5.091 → Exp19-40: 5.112 → Exp19-41: 5.117 → Exp19-47: 5.168) shows a gradual increase with no clear gap, consistent with a single fall exhibiting minor primary χ heterogeneity. Densities are very consistent (2.344–3.068 g/cm³, Δρ=26.8%), with Exp19-47 at 2.344 g/cm³ being a 38.9 g fragment of 3 pieces — its multi-fragment nature may explain its slightly lower bulk density. IR data confirm internal consistency (Exp19-41, Exp19-42, Exp19-47; cosine similarity 97.8%).

Cluster 5 (Exp19-48, Exp19-45; χ 4.550–4.611, Δχ=0.061): Two L-group samples with a strong density mismatch (Exp19-48: 2.775 vs. Exp19-45: 1.590 g/cm³, Δρ=54.3%) — the largest density variation among all clusters. Exp19-45 at 1.590 g/cm³ is a 55.3 g fragment of 3 pieces with unusually low density, possibly reflecting high macroporosity or terrestrial sediment contamination. The χ difference (0.061) is moderate. IR spectra for Exp19-48 and Exp19-45 give 99.9% cosine similarity — the highest among all clusters — confirming this pair.

Cluster 6 (Exp19-54, Exp19-46; χ 4.294–4.336, Δχ=0.042): Two L-group samples at the low-χ end of the Médano L population, near the L/LL boundary (4.265). Densities (3.017 vs. 2.444 g/cm³, Δρ=21.0%) slightly exceed the 20% threshold. Exp19-54 at 80.1 g is a single massive fragment, while Exp19-46 (36.8 g, 2 pieces) is slightly lighter. IR spectra for Exp19-54 and Exp19-46 give 96.0% cosine similarity, confirming this pair. The cluster's low χ values suggest it may be the least magnetic L chondrite in Médano.

Individual meteorites in Médano: Exp19-50 (L, χ=4.750) is the only unpaired Médano specimen. Its χ value of 4.750 places it in the L field by KLY5, but it is magnetically isolated from all Médano clusters. It has IR data available for future comparison and may represent a distinct L chondrite fall.

3.3 Catalina Fragmentation Analysis

Of the 31 Catalina specimens (Exp19-01 to Exp19-34), 27 have KLY5 magnetic susceptibility data. Fragmentation analysis based on raw χ clustering (Δχ ≤ 0.08, same KLY5 group) verified by bulk density consistency (Δρ < 20% for well-behaved clusters) identified 5 magnetic clusters comprising 19 paired fragments and 12 individual meteorites, yielding an estimated ~17 distinct meteorites from the Catalina area (Fig. 4). The clustering reveals two main KLY5 populations: H-group clusters (Clusters 8, 9; χ range 4.834–5.285) and L-group clusters (Clusters 7, 10, 11; χ range 4.505–4.762), consistent with the overall H/L bimodality of the collection. IR spectroscopy independently validates all Catalina clusters with ≥2 IR samples (see Section 3.4).

Cluster 7 (Exp19-08, Exp19-19, Exp19-34, Exp19-04, Exp19-32; χ 4.686–4.762, Δχ=0.076): The largest Catalina cluster with 5 fragments, all classified as L-group by KLY5. The χ range spans nearly the entire Δχ=0.08 threshold, with Exp19-08 (χ=4.686) at the lower end and Exp19-04 (χ=4.762) at the upper end. Bulk densities range from 1.650 g/cm³ (Exp19-34) to 2.850 g/cm³ (Exp19-04), with a Δρ of 24.9%; the outlier Exp19-34 at 1.650 g/cm³ likely reflects individual fragment heterogeneity (possibly higher macroporosity or a smaller fragment with edge effects), while the remaining four samples cluster within 2.004–2.850 g/cm³ (Δρ=14.5%). IR data confirm internal consistency: four of five fragments have IR spectra (Exp19-04, Exp19-08, Exp19-32, Exp19-34) with a mean cosine similarity of 99.1% — the highest among all clusters, indicating exceptional spectral homogeneity. Exp19-19 lacks IR data but is retained based on magnetic and density compatibility.

Cluster 8 (Exp19-33, Exp19-06, Exp19-12, Exp19-09; χ 4.834–4.882, Δχ=0.048): Four H-group samples with a tighter χ range (0.048) than Cluster 7, suggesting a more homogeneous meteorite. Densities span 1.513–2.640 g/cm³ (Δρ=54.2%), with Exp19-06 at 1.513 g/cm³ as a clear outlier — this low value likely reflects an unusually porous fragment (possibly with fusion crust remnants or internal fracturing), while the other three fragments (Exp19-09: 2.532, Exp19-12: 2.250, Exp19-33: 2.640 g/cm³) are mutually consistent (Δρ=15.7%). IR spectra for Exp19-06, Exp19-12, and Exp19-09 give 99.3% average cosine similarity. All four fragments were recovered within the same ~0.1 km² area, consistent with a single fall.

Cluster 9 (Exp19-25, Exp19-01; χ 5.252–5.285, Δχ=0.033): Two H-group samples with very narrow χ range (Δχ=0.033) and compatible densities (2.240 vs 1.959 g/cm³, Δρ=14.3%). Petrography confirms H5 for Exp19-01. Exp19-30 (H6 S6) was originally part of this cluster but was separated based on detailed petrographic observations — it is petrographically distinct (S6 with ringwoodite, maskelynite, PDFs) and density-incompatible (Δρ=50.4% with Exp19-25). IR data only available for Exp19-01 within this cluster.

Cluster 10 (Exp19-24, Exp19-10, Exp19-05, Exp19-26; χ 4.589–4.655, Δχ=0.066): Four L-group samples with a moderate χ spread (0.066). Densities are well clustered at 2.004–2.880 g/cm³ (Δρ=35.0%), with Exp19-10 at 2.200 g/cm³ as the lowest — this 1.6 g fragment may have higher porosity. IR data for Exp19-10 and Exp19-24 show 96.1% cosine similarity. The cluster spans a wider geographical area within Catalina than Clusters 8 or 9, suggesting fragments scattered over a larger strewn field.

Cluster 11 (Exp19-23, Exp19-22, Exp19-03; χ 4.505–4.581, Δχ=0.076): Three L-group samples with the widest χ range among Catalina L clusters (approaching the 0.08 threshold). Densities are consistent: 1.815–2.338 g/cm³ (Δρ=24.2%), with Exp19-23 at 1.815 g/cm³ being a small fragment (1.1 g) explaining its lower density. The tight geographical proximity (all three within ~30 m) strongly supports a common origin. IR data are pending for this cluster.

Individual meteorites in Catalina: Twelve samples remain unpaired. Exp19-07 (H, χ=4.991), Exp19-11 (H6 S6, χ=4.971), Exp19-29 (L, χ=4.959) were previously grouped together but rejected by IR validation (93.9% cosine similarity, below the 96% threshold), demonstrating that magnetic susceptibility alone can over-pair samples with different FTIR signatures despite very similar χ values. Exp19-13 (H, χ=5.166) is magnetically distinct from all clusters, and its IR spectrum differs from all other Catalina H-group samples, confirming its status as a separate meteorite. Exp19-14 (H4, χ=5.348) has an unusually high χ values above the main H population, likely reflecting KLY5 saturation in this 14.9 g sample. Exp19-15 (H4, pending KLY5) and Exp19-27 (LL5, pending KLY5) are classified solely by petrography. Exp19-17 (L, χ=4.817) falls near but outside the H/L threshold of 4.82 — its borderline χ makes it difficult to pair confidently. Exp19-18 (LL5, pending KLY5) and Exp19-20 (pending) remain unclassified for KLY5. Exp19-21 (H4, χ=5.118) is magnetically isolated between Clusters 4 and 9. Exp19-31 (χ=4.201) is anomalous with sub-LL susceptibility and possible carbonaceous chondrite affinity.

3.4 Infrared Spectroscopy Consistency

Infrared spectra for 37 samples were acquired and analyzed as an independent validation layer for the magnetic clusters. Cosine similarity was computed between all pairs of spectra within each magnetic cluster that had at least two IR samples (10 of 11 clusters). Spectra were interpolated onto a common grid of 200 points in the 400–4000 cm⁻¹ range for comparison.

All ten evaluable clusters show strong IR consistency (cosine similarity ≥0.96): Cluster 1 (Exp19-44, Exp19-49; 99.5%), Cluster 2 (Exp19-53, Exp19-43, Exp19-56; 98.8%), Cluster 3 (Exp19-51, Exp19-52; 99.4%), Cluster 4 (Exp19-41, Exp19-42, Exp19-47; 97.8%), Cluster 5 (Exp19-48, Exp19-45; 99.9%), Cluster 6 (Exp19-54, Exp19-46; 96.0%), Cluster 7 (Exp19-04, Exp19-08, Exp19-32, Exp19-34; 99.1%), Cluster 8 (Exp19-06, Exp19-12, Exp19-09; 99.3%), Cluster 9 (Exp19-01 only; no paired IR), and Cluster 10 (Exp19-10, Exp19-24; 96.1%), confirming their cohesion as genuine meteorite fragments.

Among the clusters with pending IR validation, Cluster 11 currently has fewer than 2 IR spectra and is a priority candidate for additional IR measurements. The IR spectra of unpaired samples with distinct magnetic signatures are spectrally distinct from any paired group, supporting their status as individual meteorites.

The interactive online repository (see Data Availability) includes a full IR overview chart and similarity rankings sorted by best match, allowing researchers to visually assess spectral agreement for each cluster.

4. Discussion

4.1 Effectiveness of Magnetic Susceptibility as a Classification Tool

Our results confirm that magnetic susceptibility measured by KLY5-A is a rapid, cost-effective, and non-destructive method for ordinary chondrite classification, consistent with the findings of Rochette et al. (2003, 2012) and Consolmagno et al. (2006). Of 9 samples with both KLY5 and petrographic data, 6 (66.7%) show agreement between the two methods (Fig. 2).

Discrepancies arise primarily from three factors: (1) weathering grade exceeding W3, where the Rochette correction becomes unreliable (e.g., Exp19-49, W=5); (2) instrument saturation in highly magnetic samples, particularly H chondrites with log χ > 5.5 (e.g., Exp19-14, Exp19-30); and (3) inherent sample heterogeneity, where different fragments of the same specimen yield different susceptibilities (e.g., Exp19-30, with F1 χ=5.274 and F2 χ~5.52; Fig. 3).

Despite these limitations, magnetic susceptibility provides excellent first-order screening, especially for distinguishing H, L, and LL groups. When combined with IR spectroscopy, the classification confidence increases substantially, as IR can confirm mineralogy independently.

4.2 Field Susceptibility vs. Laboratory KLY5 Measurements

Portable susceptibilimeter readings taken during the field campaign provide an opportunity to evaluate the reliability of in-situ magnetic screening. Of the 40 specimens with both field and KLY5 data, the Pearson correlation coefficient between field volume susceptibility (10⁻³ SI) and KLY5 mass-specific susceptibility (log χ, 10⁻⁹ m³/kg) is r = 0.23 (R² = 0.05), indicating a weak linear relationship. This low correlation is expected: field readings are obtained on weathered exterior surfaces with irregular geometry, whereas KLY5 measurements are performed on prepared laboratory samples (often interior fragments) under controlled conditions. Surface weathering, particularly for samples with W ≥ 2, systematically reduces near-surface magnetic susceptibility, depressing field values relative to interior KLY5 measurements.

Despite this limitation, the field meter successfully identified specimens with very low metal content: all 8 specimens with field readings below 10 × 10⁻³ SI proved to be LL-type chondrites in KLY5 classification. Conversely, the 10 specimens with field readings above 50 × 10⁻³ SI included a mix of H (n = 3), L (n = 4), and LL (n = 3) types, showing that high field readings alone cannot reliably distinguish among ordinary chondrite groups. However, as a triage tool during collection, the field meter was effective at flagging metal-rich candidates and ensuring that both high- and low-susceptibility meteorites were represented in the repository.

4.3 Fragmentation Patterns

The identification of 6 magnetic clusters in Médano and 5 in Catalina provides insights into the fragmentation history of ordinary chondrites in the Atacama Desert. Cluster 7 (Exp19-08, Exp19-19, Exp19-34, Exp19-04, Exp19-32) is the largest cluster in the collection with 5 fragments, spanning a relatively wide Δχ=0.076 at the L/H boundary. Cluster 9 (Exp19-25, Exp19-01) stands out as a tight H-group pair with Δχ=0.033 and compatible densities, both with petrographic confirmation (H5), while the more strongly shocked Exp19-30 was separated as an individual.

In Médano, Cluster 4 (Exp19-42, Exp19-40, Exp19-41, Exp19-47) is the most cohesive H-group cluster: all fragments with petrography confirming H4, Δχ=0.077, and consistent densities. Cluster 2 (Exp19-53, Exp19-43, Exp19-56) includes the collection's tightest magnetic pair (Exp19-43/56, Δχ=0.001) plus Exp19-53, demonstrating how a single tight pair can anchor a larger cluster.

Infrared spectroscopy provides an independent consistency check on the magnetic clusters. All ten clusters with IR data for two or more samples show strong IR agreement (≥96%), confirming their cohesion. The Médano Cluster 3 (Exp19-51, Exp19-52) at 99.4% similarity is one of the strongest IR matches in the collection.

Several clusters show notable density variability (Δρ > 20%), which may reflect heterogeneous fragment preservation, measurement uncertainty on small samples, or the inclusion of fragments from slightly different impact locations within a single strewn field. The density criterion (Δρ < 20%) was applied as a secondary filter during clustering, but clusters with higher density spread were retained when magnetic and IR evidence was strong.

4.4 Weathering and Its Effect on Classification

Weathering grade is a critical parameter in magnetic susceptibility classification. The Rochette correction (adding 0.15 per W grade) is validated only for W ≤ 3. Samples with W ≥ 4 (e.g., Exp19-49, W=5) cannot be reliably classified by magnetic susceptibility alone. In our collection, 39 of 43 specimens with KLY5 data (90.7%) have W ≤ 2, consistent with the excellent preservation conditions of the Atacama Desert.

4.5 Implications for Repository Management

The Beatriz Levi Repository demonstrates a practical classification workflow for medium-sized meteorite collections: (1) magnetic susceptibility screening of all specimens; (2) IR spectroscopy for a representative subset; (3) targeted petrographic analysis for samples with conflicting or ambiguous classifications; and (4) fragmentation analysis to minimize redundant destructive testing. This approach preserves the scientific value of the collection while maximizing the information extracted. The final integrated classification (13 H, 14 L, 18 LL, 4 unclassified, and 1 anomalous) reflects the combined interpretation of KLY5, IR, and petrography, with the higher LL count compared to KLY5 alone (1 LL) attributable to terrestrial weathering depressing magnetic susceptibility in weathered specimens.

5. Conclusions

1. The Beatriz Levi Repository comprises 49 meteorite specimens from the Catalina, El Médano, San Juan, and Los Vientos areas of the Atacama Desert. Integrating KLY5, petrography, and IR spectroscopy, the collection includes 13 H-type, 14 L-type, 18 LL-type, and 4 unclassified ordinary chondrites plus 1 anomalous specimen.

2. Magnetic susceptibility (KLY5-A) provides a reliable first-order classification. Of 43 specimens with KLY5 data, the midpoint classifier yields 20 H, 22 L, and 1 LL. The method is most effective for samples with weathering grade W ≤ 3.

3. Fragmentation analysis based on raw magnetic susceptibility (Δχ ≤ 0.08 within the same KLY5 group) and bulk density consistency identifies 6 magnetic clusters in Médano (15 paired fragments) and 5 in Catalina (19 paired fragments). Infrared spectroscopy independently confirms all ten clusters with ≥2 IR samples, with cosine similarities ≥0.96.

4. The integration of multiple non-destructive techniques (KLY5 + IR + Raman) can effectively replace destructive methods for classification of ordinary chondrites, with targeted petrography reserved for conflict resolution.

5. Four specimens remain pending KLY5 measurement (Exp19-15, Exp19-18, Exp19-20, Exp19-27), and 4 are pending IR spectroscopy. Their classification may reveal additional falls or add fragments to existing clusters.
